1. Random variables, distributions, and moments

A random variable turns outcomes into numbers

A random variable is a rule

\[ X:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb R. \]

Example: toss a coin twice and let \(X\) be the number of heads.

\[ \begin{array}{c|cccc} \text{Outcome} & TT & HT & TH & HH\\ \hline X & 0 & 1 & 1 & 2 \end{array} \]

Explain that the outcome can be a word or a sequence, while the random variable assigns it a numerical value that we can analyze.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 22-24.

Discrete example 1: the number of heads has a PMF

For the number of heads in two fair tosses:

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccc} x & 0 & 1 & 2\\ \hline P(X=x) & 1/4 & 1/2 & 1/4 \end{array} \]

The probabilities must satisfy

\[ p_X(x)\ge0, \qquad \sum_xp_X(x)=1. \]

This table is the probability mass function (PMF). Link each probability back to the four equally likely coin outcomes.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 22-25.

Discrete example 2: Bernoulli

A Bernoulli random variable records success as 1 and failure as 0:

\[ X=\begin{cases} 1, & \text{success},\\ 0, & \text{failure}. \end{cases} \]

If \(X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\), then

\[ p_X(1)=p, \qquad p_X(0)=1-p. \]

Example: \(X=1\) if a student passes and \(X=0\) otherwise.

This is the simplest discrete distribution. Emphasize that the PMF gives a probability to each possible value.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), p. 56.

Exercise 1: read a PMF

Find:

  1. \(P(X=1)\);
  2. \(P(X\ge1)\);
  3. \(P(X\le1)\).

\(P(X=1)=1/2\). \(P(X\ge1)=P(X=1)+P(X=2)=3/4\). \(P(X\le1)=P(X=0)+P(X=1)=3/4\).

Discrete variables use a PMF; continuous variables use a PDF

Discrete random variable

Takes separated values.

Examples: number of heads, number of children.

Use a probability mass function (PMF):

\[ p_X(x)=P(X=x). \]

\[ p_X(x)\ge0, \quad \sum_xp_X(x)=1. \]

Continuous random variable

Can take any value in an interval.

Examples: height, time, income.

Use a probability density function (PDF):

\[ f_X(x)\ge0. \]

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=1. \]

Do not develop integrals on this slide. The main distinction is separated values versus a continuum of values.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 22-39.

For a PDF, area represents probability

For a continuous random variable,

\[ \boxed{P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx}. \]

The probability is the area under the PDF between \(a\) and \(b\).

At one exact value, there is no width and therefore no area:

\[P(X=x)=0.\]

Say: the vertical height is density, but the area is probability. A tall curve at one point does not itself give a probability. Probability needs an interval with positive width.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 29-39.

The shaded area is an interval probability

Explain that the height of the curve is density, not probability at one exact point. Probability over an interval is the shaded area.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 29-39; figure generated in R.

The Uniform PDF has constant height

If \(X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(a,b)\), then

\[ f_X(x)=\begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{b-a},&a\le x\le b,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

The height is \(1/(b-a)\) so that the total rectangular area is

\[ (b-a)\frac{1}{b-a}=1. \]

Draw the rectangular PDF. Its width is \(b-a\), so its height must be \(1/(b-a)\) for the total area to equal one.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 36-39.

Example: calculate a Uniform probability

Suppose

\[X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,10).\]

Find \(P(2\le X\le5)\).

The PDF height is

\[\frac{1}{10-0}=0.1.\]

Therefore,

\[ P(2\le X\le5) =(5-2)(0.1) =0.3. \]

Probability is the rectangular area: interval width 3 times PDF height 0.1. Also point out that \(P(X=2)=0\) even though \(f_X(2)=0.1\).

[Sources] - Instructor-developed example based on Hansen (2021), pp. 36-39.

Continuous example 2: Normal

Write \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), where \(\mu\) sets the center and \(\sigma\) sets the spread.

The red curve moves the mean from zero to two without changing the variance. The green curve keeps mean zero but increases the variance from one to four, making the distribution more spread out.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 61, 113-116; figure generated in R.

Linear transformations of a Normal variable stay Normal

Suppose

\[X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2).\]

For constants \(a\) and \(b\),

\[ \boxed{a+bX\sim N\!\left(a+b\mu,\;b^2\sigma^2\right)}. \]

Important special cases:

\[ X+a\sim N(\mu+a,\sigma^2), \qquad bX\sim N(b\mu,b^2\sigma^2). \]

Adding a constant moves the center but does not change the variance. Multiplying by \(b\) multiplies the mean by \(b\) and the variance by \(b^2\).

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 61, 113-116.

Standardization is a Normal transformation

If

\[X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),\]

subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation:

\[ \boxed{Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)}. \]

The standardized value tells us how many standard deviations \(X\) is above or below its mean.

Connect this directly to the preceding transformation formula by taking \(a=-\mu/\sigma\) and \(b=1/\sigma\).

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 113-116.

Example: a Normal interval after standardizing

Exam scores satisfy

\[X\sim N(70,10^2).\]

Let \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and suppose you are given

\[P(-1\le Z\le1)=0.6827.\]

Find \(P(60\le X\le80)\).

Pause here and ask students what transformation would turn \(X\) into a standard Normal variable.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed example.

Example solution: standardize both endpoints

Standardize both endpoints:

\[ \frac{60-70}{10}=-1, \qquad \frac{80-70}{10}=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} P(60\le X\le80) &=P(-1\le Z\le1)\\ &=0.6827. \end{aligned} \]

First identify the mean and standard deviation. Standardize each endpoint, then use the standard-Normal probability supplied in the question.

Suppose

Let \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and suppose

Expectation is a probability-weighted average

For a discrete random variable,

\[ E(X)=\sum_x xP(X=x). \]

Expectation is the long-run average value across many repetitions.

It does not need to be one of the values that \(X\) can actually take.

Distinguish the expected value from the most likely value. It is a center of the distribution, formed by weighting each value by its probability.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 25-28, 37-42.

Example: expected number of heads

For two fair tosses,

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccc} x & 0 & 1 & 2\\ \hline P(X=x) & 1/4 & 1/2 & 1/4 \end{array} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} E(X) &=0\left(\frac14\right) +1\left(\frac12\right) +2\left(\frac14\right)\\ &=1. \end{aligned} \]

Read the calculation aloud as “value times probability, then add.”

[Sources] - Example constructed from Hansen (2021), pp. 25-28.

Exercise 4: calculate an expectation

A simple investment has payoff \(X\):

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccc} x & -2 & 0 & 4\\ \hline P(X=x) & 0.25 & 0.50 & 0.25 \end{array} \]

  1. Verify that the probabilities sum to one.
  2. Find \(E(X)\).
  3. Is the expected payoff equal to the most likely payoff?

The probabilities sum to \(0.25+0.50+0.25=1\). \(E(X)=(-2)(0.25)+(0)(0.50)+(4)(0.25)=0.5\). The most likely payoff is 0, while the expected payoff is 0.5. The expected value does not need to be a value that \(X\) can take.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed exercise based on Hansen (2021), pp. 25-28.

Expectation is linear

For constants \(a\) and \(b\),

\[ E(a+bX)=a+bE(X). \]

For two random variables,

\[ E(X+Y)=E(X)+E(Y), \]

whether or not \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.

In general, \(E[g(X)]\ne g(E[X])\).

Use a wage conversion example: if annual earnings equal 12 times monthly earnings, expected annual earnings equal 12 times expected monthly earnings.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 25-28, 40-42.

Variance measures spread around the mean

Let \(\mu=E(X)\). Then

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]. \]

An equivalent formula is

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2. \]

The standard deviation is

\[ \operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}. \]

Variance is an average squared distance from the mean. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling. Standard deviation is in the original units.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 40-42.

Example: variance of the number of heads

We already found \(E(X)=1\).

\[ E(X^2) =0^2\left(\frac14\right) +1^2\left(\frac12\right) +2^2\left(\frac14\right) =\frac32. \]

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X) =E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2 =\frac32-1^2 =\frac12. \]

Keep the distinction between \(E(X^2)\) and \([E(X)]^2\) visible.

[Sources] - Example constructed from Hansen (2021), pp. 40-42.

Exercise 5: expectation and variance

Use the investment payoff:

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccc} x & -2 & 0 & 4\\ \hline P(X=x) & 0.25 & 0.50 & 0.25 \end{array} \]

Given \(E(X)=0.5\):

  1. calculate \(E(X^2)\);
  2. calculate \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\);
  3. calculate \(\operatorname{SD}(X)\).

\(E(X^2)=4(0.25)+0(0.50)+16(0.25)=5\). \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=5-(0.5)^2=4.75\). \(\operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{4.75}\approx2.179\).

[Sources] - Instructor-developed exercise based on Hansen (2021), pp. 40-42.

A shift changes the mean but not the variance

For constants \(a\) and \(b\),

\[ E(a+bX)=a+bE(X), \]

\[ \operatorname{Var}(a+bX)=b^2\operatorname{Var}(X). \]

Adding \(a\) shifts every value equally, so spread does not change.

Multiplying by \(b\) changes every distance from the mean by \(|b|\).

Contrast the two effects. A location shift changes the center; a scale change affects both center and spread.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 40-42.

Covariance describes how two variables move together

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) =E[(X-E(X))(Y-E(Y))]. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E(XY)-E(X)E(Y). \]

Positive: they tend to move in the same direction.
Negative: they tend to move in opposite directions.

Say that covariance combines two deviations. When both deviations have the same sign, their product is positive.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 91-93.

Correlation puts covariance on a common scale

\[ \operatorname{Corr}(X,Y) =\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)} {\operatorname{SD}(X)\operatorname{SD}(Y)}. \]

\[-1\le\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y)\le1.\]

Correlation measures the direction and strength of a linear relationship.

Covariance depends on units; correlation does not. Zero correlation does not rule out a nonlinear relationship.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 91-94.

Correlation changes the shape of a scatter plot

Ask students to describe direction before naming correlation. The middle panel has no clear linear direction.

[Sources] - Hansen (2021), pp. 91-94; simulated figures generated in R.

Exercise 6: covariance and correlation

Three equally likely observations are

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccc} &1&2&3\\ \hline X&1&2&3\\ Y&1&3&2 \end{array} \]

Treat each column as having probability \(1/3\).

  1. Find \(E(X)\) and \(E(Y)\).
  2. Find \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\).
  3. Find \(\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y)\).

\(E(X)=E(Y)=2\). \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=[(-1)(-1)+0(1)+(1)(0)]/3=1/3\). \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\operatorname{Var}(Y)=2/3\). Therefore \(\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y)=(1/3)/(2/3)=1/2\).

2. Regression: estimating a line

What is regression?

Regression uses a line to summarize how an outcome \(Y\) changes with an explanatory variable \(X\):

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i. \]

  • \(\beta_0\): intercept;
  • \(\beta_1\): slope;
  • \(u_i\): the part of \(Y_i\) not described by the line.

From data, we estimate the line

\[ \widehat Y_i=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1X_i. \]

Say: “Regression draws one line through a cloud of points. The line cannot usually pass through every point, so we need a rule for choosing the best line. That rule will be ordinary least squares.”

[Sources] - Instructor-developed introduction to linear regression.

Which line is best? What rule should decide?

Ask students to vote for Line A, B, or C. Do not reveal the answer yet. Then ask what “best” should mean. Visual judgment is not enough when many lines look plausible. The next slide introduces a numerical rule that can compare every possible line.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed data and R figure.

Ordinary Least Squares minimizes squared residuals

For a candidate line \(b_0+b_1X_i\), the residual is

\[ e_i=Y_i-(b_0+b_1X_i). \]

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) chooses \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) to minimize

\[ S(b_0,b_1) =\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 =\sum_{i=1}^n\left[Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i\right]^2. \]

Why square the residuals? Positive and negative errors cannot cancel, and large errors receive more weight.

Say: “Every possible line produces residuals. We square them, add them, and choose the line with the smallest total.” Connect this directly to unconstrained minimization from calculus.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed OLS explanation.

Step 1: differentiate the OLS objective

The two first-order conditions are

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial b_0} =-2\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i)=0, \]

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial b_1} =-2\sum_{i=1}^nX_i(Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i)=0. \]

The first condition gives

\[ \widehat\beta_0=\overline Y-\widehat\beta_1\overline X. \]

Differentiate one squared residual on the board first. For the intercept, the inner derivative is \(-1\); for the slope, it is \(-X_i\). Divide the first condition by \(n\) to obtain the intercept formula.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed scalar OLS derivation.

Step 2: solve for the slope and intercept

Substitute \(\widehat\beta_0=\overline Y-\widehat\beta_1\overline X\) into the second first-order condition:

\[ \boxed{ \widehat\beta_1 =\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\overline X)(Y_i-\overline Y)} {\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\overline X)^2}} \]

Then calculate

\[ \boxed{\widehat\beta_0 =\overline Y-\widehat\beta_1\overline X}. \]

The fitted regression line is \(\widehat Y_i=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat\beta_1X_i\).

Say: “The slope compares how X and Y move together with how much X moves by itself. Once we know the slope, the intercept makes the fitted line pass through \((\overline X,\overline Y)\).”

[Sources] - Instructor-developed scalar OLS derivation.

Example: study hours and exam scores

Suppose we observe four students:

\[ \begin{array}{c|rrrr} i&1&2&3&4\\ \hline X_i\text{ (hours)}&1&2&3&4\\ Y_i\text{ (score)}&52&53&55&56 \end{array} \]

Calculate the OLS slope and intercept.

First,

\[ \overline X=2.5, \qquad \overline Y=54. \]

Reveal only the means after students have calculated them. Then build the deviation table on the next slide.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed example and data.

Example solution: plug the data into the formulas

\[ \begin{array}{c|rr|rr|rr} i&X_i&Y_i&X_i-\bar X&Y_i-\bar Y& (X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)&(X_i-\bar X)^2\\ \hline 1&1&52&-1.5&-2&3&2.25\\ 2&2&53&-0.5&-1&0.5&0.25\\ 3&3&55&0.5&1&0.5&0.25\\ 4&4&56&1.5&2&3&2.25\\ \hline &&&&&7&5 \end{array} \]

\[ \widehat\beta_1=\frac75=1.4, \qquad \widehat\beta_0=54-(1.4)(2.5)=50.5. \]

\[ \boxed{\widehat Y=50.5+1.4X} \]

Interpret the slope: one additional study hour is associated with a 1.4 point increase in the fitted exam score. At two hours, the fitted score is \(50.5+1.4(2)=53.3\).

Exercise 7: OLS without an intercept

Suppose the fitted line is forced through the origin:

\[ Y_i=\beta X_i+u_i. \]

  1. Starting from \(S(b)=\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-bX_i)^2\), differentiate with respect to \(b\) and show that

    \[\widehat\beta=\frac{\sum_iX_iY_i}{\sum_iX_i^2}.\]

  2. Use \(X=(1,2,3)\) and \(Y=(2,3,5)\) to calculate \(\widehat\beta\) and write the fitted line.

Differentiate: \(dS/db=-2\sum_iX_i(Y_i-bX_i)=0\). Therefore \(\widehat\beta=\sum_iX_iY_i/\sum_iX_i^2\). For the data, \(\sum_iX_iY_i=2+6+15=23\) and \(\sum_iX_i^2=1+4+9=14\). Hence \(\widehat\beta=23/14\approx1.643\) and \(\widehat Y=(23/14)X\).

[Sources] - Instructor-developed exercise.

Multiple regression uses matrices

With several explanatory variables,

\[ \mathbf y=\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta+\mathbf u. \]

\[ \mathbf y= \begin{bmatrix} y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n \end{bmatrix}_{n\times1}, \qquad \mathbf u= \begin{bmatrix} u_1\\u_2\\\vdots\\u_n \end{bmatrix}_{n\times1}. \]

Each row is one observation.

\[ \boldsymbol\beta= \begin{bmatrix} \beta_0\\\beta_1\\\vdots\\\beta_{k-1} \end{bmatrix}_{k\times1}. \]

Each entry is one coefficient.

Start with dimensions. The outcome vector and error vector contain one number for each observation. The coefficient vector contains one number for each column of the design matrix.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed matrix notation for multiple regression.

The design matrix contains the explanatory variables

\[ \mathbf X= \begin{bmatrix} 1&X_{11}&X_{12}&\cdots&X_{1,k-1}\\ 1&X_{21}&X_{22}&\cdots&X_{2,k-1}\\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 1&X_{n1}&X_{n2}&\cdots&X_{n,k-1} \end{bmatrix}_{n\times k}. \]

  • one row for each observation;
  • one column for each coefficient;
  • the first column of ones produces the intercept \(\beta_0\).

For observation \(i\), the matrix equation reproduces

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_{i1}+\cdots+\beta_{k-1}X_{i,k-1}+u_i. \]

Point across one row of X and then down the coefficient vector. Their dot product gives the fitted value for that observation.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed matrix notation for multiple regression.

Matrix OLS: write and expand the objective

OLS minimizes

\[ S(\mathbf b) =(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\mathbf b)' (\mathbf y-\mathbf X\mathbf b). \]

Expand the product:

\[ S(\mathbf b) =\mathbf y'\mathbf y -2\mathbf b'\mathbf X'\mathbf y +\mathbf b'\mathbf X'\mathbf X\mathbf b. \]

Matrix derivative rules

\[ \frac{\partial(\mathbf b'\mathbf c)}{\partial\mathbf b} =\mathbf c \]

\[ \frac{\partial(\mathbf b'\mathbf A\mathbf b)} {\partial\mathbf b} =(\mathbf A+\mathbf A')\mathbf b \]

If \(\mathbf A\) is symmetric, this is \(2\mathbf A\mathbf b\).

The objective is still the sum of squared residuals. Matrix notation only allows all residuals and all coefficients to be handled at once. In the quadratic term, \(X'X\) is symmetric.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed matrix OLS derivation.

Matrix OLS: differentiate and solve

Differentiate:

\[ \frac{\partial S(\mathbf b)}{\partial\mathbf b} =-2\mathbf X'\mathbf y +2\mathbf X'\mathbf X\mathbf b. \]

Set the derivative equal to zero:

\[ \mathbf X'\mathbf X\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta} =\mathbf X'\mathbf y. \]

If \(\mathbf X'\mathbf X\) is invertible,

\[ \boxed{\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta} =(\mathbf X'\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X'\mathbf y}. \]

Rules used

\[ \frac{\partial(\mathbf y'\mathbf y)}{\partial\mathbf b}=\mathbf0 \]

\[ \frac{\partial(-2\mathbf b'\mathbf X'\mathbf y)} {\partial\mathbf b}=-2\mathbf X'\mathbf y \]

\[ \frac{\partial(\mathbf b'\mathbf X'\mathbf X\mathbf b)} {\partial\mathbf b}=2\mathbf X'\mathbf X\mathbf b \]

Say: “This is the same three-step process as before: write the squared error, differentiate, and solve. The matrix inverse solves all first-order conditions simultaneously.”

[Sources] - Instructor-developed matrix OLS derivation.

Exercise 8: calculate matrix OLS

Let

\[ \mathbf X= \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 1&2\\ 1&3 \end{bmatrix}_{3\times2}, \qquad \mathbf y= \begin{bmatrix} 2\\3\\5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf X'= \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&1\\ 1&2&3 \end{bmatrix}_{2\times3}. \]

  1. Calculate \(\mathbf X'\mathbf X\) and \(\mathbf X'\mathbf y\).

For Step 2, use

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 3&6\\ 6&14 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} =\frac16 \begin{bmatrix} 14&-6\\ -6&3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

  1. Use this inverse to calculate \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf X'\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X'\mathbf y\).
  2. Write the fitted regression line.

\(X'X=\begin{bmatrix}3&6\\6&14\end{bmatrix}\) and \(X'y=\begin{bmatrix}10\\23\end{bmatrix}\). The inverse is \((X'X)^{-1}=\frac16\begin{bmatrix}14&-6\\-6&3\end{bmatrix}\). Therefore \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta} =\begin{bmatrix}1/3\\3/2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\widehat Y=1/3+(3/2)X\).

3. Hypothesis testing

Is this coin-tossing game fair?

We play the following game:

  • if heads appears more often, I win;
  • if tails appears more often, you win.

If the coin is fair, both of us should have the same chance of winning.

We toss the coin 50 times and observe

\[ \boxed{48\text{ heads and }2\text{ tails}.} \]

Does the coin still look fair?

Ask for an immediate reaction before showing any formula. Most students will say that 48 heads feels too unusual for a fair coin. The next slide turns that reaction into mathematical language.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed hypothesis-testing example.

The null hypothesis gives us a reference distribution

Start by temporarily assuming that the coin is fair:

\[ H_0:p=0.5. \]

Under \(H_0\), \(X\) counts successes in 50 independent \(\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.5)\) trials, so

\[ X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(50,0.5). \]

If \(H_0\) is true, values near 25 heads are common. Values near 0 or 50 heads are rare.

Say: “We did not begin by proving that the coin is fair. We temporarily assumed fairness. This assumption gives us a reference distribution. We can now ask whether the observed result is normal or surprising under that distribution.”

[Sources] - Instructor-developed example using the Binomial distribution.

A very small tail probability makes us doubt \(H_0\)

We observed \(X=48\). Under the fair-coin hypothesis,

\[ P(X\ge48\mid H_0) =\sum_{x=48}^{50}\binom{50}{x}(0.5)^{50} \approx1.13\times10^{-12}. \]

The observed result is extremely unlikely under \(H_0\).

Therefore, we reject the null hypothesis that the coin is fair.

This is the basic logic of a hypothesis test:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{assumption} &\longrightarrow \text{reference distribution}\\ &\longrightarrow \text{tail probability} \longrightarrow \text{decision}. \end{aligned} \]

Say: “The observed result lies in an extremely unlikely tail of the reference distribution, so the data give strong evidence against our original assumption.”

Emphasize that a low-probability event is not impossible. A hypothesis test uses the size of that probability to decide whether the original assumption is still credible. The displayed probability is a one-sided tail probability; a two-sided test would also count outcomes with at most two heads.

[Sources] - Instructor-developed example using the Binomial distribution.

An OLS \(t\)-test asks whether the slope is zero

Test whether the population slope is zero:

\[ H_0:\beta_1=0, \qquad H_1:\beta_1\ne0. \]

Statistic:

\[ t=\frac{\widehat\beta_1-0} {SE(\widehat\beta_1)}. \]

\(SE(\widehat\beta_1)\) measures the uncertainty of \(\widehat\beta_1\).

Large \(|t|\) gives evidence against \(H_0\).

Say: “The null hypothesis says that the population slope is zero. The \(t\)-statistic measures how many standard errors the estimated slope is from zero. Under the null hypothesis, values near zero are common. Values far into either tail are unusual.”

The graph shows the rejection regions for an illustrative two-sided 5% test with 20 degrees of freedom. The \(p\)-value is the probability, under \(H_0\), of obtaining a \(t\)-statistic at least as far from zero as the one we observed.
